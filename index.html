I am a PhD candidate in Strategy and Business Economics at the Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia. I study industrial organization, with a focus on competition between digital platforms and two-sided markets.

My current research uses trip-level data from the ride-hailing industry to study how driver multi-homing shapes platform competition, pricing, and welfare. I am on the academic job market in 2026–27.

Job Market Paper

Who Benefits from Platform Competition? Evidence from Ride-Hailing

Latest draft: October 2026

PDF

Abstract

Competition among digital platforms may enhance efficiency by curtailing platforms’ market power or reduce efficiency by diminishing network externalities. Furthermore, because platforms set fees on both sides jointly, the different effects of competition on market power and on network externalities can translate into opposite welfare effects across sides. I study the welfare effects of platform competition with an equilibrium model estimated on data covering the universe of ride-hailing transactions in a major Chinese city. The model incorporates platforms’ matching and pricing, drivers’ platform adoption and labor supply, and riders’ trip requests. I find that concentration can raise efficiency, by up to 3.8% relative to the status quo. However, the impact of concentration differs substantially across the sides of the market. Platforms earn more profit in a more concentrated market, while drivers lose surplus, because concentration not only increases platforms’ monopsony power but also raises drivers’ productivity, which lowers platforms’ demand for labor. Whether riders gain or lose depends crucially on the level of concentration. They gain from mild concentration but lose from severe concentration. Holding market structure fixed, promoting multi-homing improves welfare and benefits all sides of the market.

Working Papers

Algorithmic Price Discrimination

(with Yang Yu)

Abstract

Modern pricing algorithms exploit high-dimensional consumer information, making price discrimination subtle and difficult for consumers and regulators to detect. We propose a machine-learning-based method to test whether a given piece of consumer information is used to price-discriminate. Built on a double/debiased machine-learning framework, the method removes bias from model misspecification and overfitting. We apply it to test whether a ride-hailing platform charges frequent riders differently from occasional riders. A naive linear analysis produces persistent false positives, whereas our method finds no statistically significant difference in prices across riders.

Dual Role on Shoulders: Who Benefits from Platform Exclusivity?

Abstract

A vertically integrated digital platform plays a dual role: it operates a marketplace for third-party products while selling its own first-party products on that marketplace. This paper examines whether such a platform should keep its first-party products exclusive to its own marketplace, and what this controversial practice implies for welfare. Using a model of Nash bargaining and Bertrand competition, I show that giving up exclusivity raises platform profits but lowers consumer surplus. The rationale is that selling first-party products on rival platforms serves as a collusive device: it softens competition for platform usage and ultimately leads to higher product prices.
